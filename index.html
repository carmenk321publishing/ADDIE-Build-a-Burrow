An ADDIE workspace

Every good course starts as a hole in a hill.

ADDIE is just five sensible steps for building anything people need to learn. Work through them here by building a hillside burrow, one phase at a time, and leave with a design outline for a course of your own. Nothing is graded and nothing is locked — the review notes exist to sharpen your thinking, not to score it.

The process, in four short steps: 1. Explore and choose 2. Build 3. Review 4. Continue

The five ADDIE phases you will work through

Phase 1 of 5 — the foundation

Analyze

Before anyone digs, work out what's actually wrong. The most common mistake in this whole field is starting with "we need a course" instead of "here's the gap, and here's why it exists." Sometimes the honest answer is a one-page job aid, a manager conversation, or fixing a broken tool.

Things worth considering
  • Name the gap: what people do now vs. what they should do.
  • Ask why the gap exists — knowledge, skill, motivation, tools, or process? Only the first two are training problems.
  • Describe the audience concretely: how many, what they already know, how much time they have.
  • Decide the smallest thing that could work: job aid, email, 5-minute microlesson, or a full course.
  • Write down how you'd know the problem was solved. That sentence becomes your evaluation plan later.

Try the shape: "Right now, [who] [does X], which causes [consequence]. It's happening because [cause]."

Phase 2 of 5 — the blueprint

Design

Now draw the plan. Design is where you decide what learners will be able to do afterwards — the goal, not the activity. Keep the "what" separate from the "how", because there should be more than one route to the same door.

Things worth considering
  • Write objectives as Condition + Action + Standard: "Given a live class, share a single window without exposing other tabs, in under 30 seconds."
  • Start with a real Bloom's verb — identify, apply, analyse, evaluate, create. Never "understand", "know", or "be aware of"; you can't see those happen.
  • A goal is not an instruction. "Watch the video" is an activity; "select the correct sharing mode" is a goal.
  • UDL: you can't control learner variability, only how flexible your design is. Plan more than one way in.
  • Plan your formative checks here — the little quizzes, reflections and practice moments woven through the course.

Phase 3 of 5 — the build

Develop

Time to actually build the burrow. You choose the materials — video, text, practice, audio — and you make sure everybody can get through the door. Prototype small, test early, and don't build forty screens before someone else has looked at two.

Things worth considering
  • Storyboard first. One rough screen-by-screen sketch saves a week of rebuilding.
  • Chunk self-paced content into 5–15 minute segments with a check at each seam.
  • Vary the vehicles. The point isn't "learning styles" — it's that one single format will always fail somebody.
  • Pilot with two or three real learners before launch. Watch where they hesitate, don't just ask if they liked it.
  • Keep slides sparse: a handful of words per line, readable across a room.

Phase 4 of 5 — moving in

Implement

The burrow is built; now the neighbours arrive. Implementation is all the unglamorous logistics that decide whether anyone actually completes your course: who launches it, where it lives, when, and who needs to know.

Things worth considering
  • If someone else will facilitate, they need a train-the-trainer pass and a facilitator guide.
  • Prepare the environment: LMS enrolment, links, permissions, a tech check for live sessions.
  • Tell learners why before they click in. A one-line "what's in it for you" lifts completion more than any animation.
  • Name your stakeholders and your go-live date. Vague launch dates never launch.
  • Start collecting data on day one — where people drop out is your best redesign brief.

Requester, approver, facilitators, IT, anyone who just needs to be kept in the loop.

Phase 5 of 5 — does the roof hold?

Evaluate

Last phase, and the one most people shrink to a smiley-face survey. Formative evaluation is the checking you do during; summative is the proof after. Both should point back at the objectives you wrote in Design.

Things worth considering
  • Four useful levels to think in: did they like it, did they learn it, did they do it at work, did anything change for the organisation.
  • Most teams stop at "did they like it". One level up is where the real information lives.
  • For content knowledge, offer more than one way to show it: a quiz, a short recording, a worked example, a submitted artefact.
  • Skill-based assessment is narrower by nature — but you can still vary the context, not just the format.
  • Close the loop: write down what you'd change next time while you still remember.

A course can build skill and confidence. Whether people keep doing it usually depends on their manager.

Bloom's verb reference

Climb the pyramid. Lower-order thinking builds the ground floor; higher-order thinking is where people actually use what they know. Pick the verb that matches the thinking you want — and never write "understand".

Createdesign, compose, plan, construct, produce, devise, adapt
Evaluatejudge, critique, justify, recommend, prioritise, defend
Analyzecompare, contrast, differentiate, organise, attribute, diagnose
Applydemonstrate, use, carry out, solve, implement, perform
Understand and explaindescribe, summarise, paraphrase, classify, interpret, illustrate
Rememberidentify, list, name, recall, label, define, select

Verbs to avoid in objectives

understand · know · be aware of · appreciate · learn about · be familiar with · realise — you can't observe any of these, so you can't assess them. Ask "what would I see them do if they understood?" and use that verb instead.

UDL quick reference

Universal Design for Learning starts from one honest premise: learners vary, and you can't control that variability — only how flexible your design is. Build the flexibility in from the start rather than retrofitting it for the person who struggles.

Multiple means of engagement

The "why" of learning. Give choice, make relevance obvious, keep the challenge at the right level, and build in ways for learners to keep going when it gets hard.

Multiple means of representation

The "what" of learning. Same content, more than one route in: text plus audio, diagram plus explanation, key terms defined on the spot, captions on everything.

Multiple means of action and expression

The "how" of learning. Let people show what they know in more than one way — write it, record it, demonstrate it, build it.

The design habit to keep

Separate the goal from the method. "Summarise the safety procedure" is a goal; "write a 200-word paragraph" is one method of many. Fix the goal, open up the method — unless the method is the skill.

Scaffold for heavy reading

For dense professional content — legal, medical, technical, compliance — don't hand over the wall of text and hope. Walk learners through it in four moves.

  1. Preview questions. Two or three questions before they read, so they know what they're looking for and activate what they already know.
  2. Click and chunk. Break the text into small pieces. Unknown word? Look it up, don't guess past it. Keep a running list of key terms beside the text.
  3. Analyse and question. Go back in: what's the claim, what's the evidence, what does this mean for my job? Learners generate their own questions here, not just answer yours.
  4. Discuss, then show. Pair or group discussion to test understanding out loud, then a short task that proves they can use it.

Pairs well with CLIL

This is the same logic as content-and-language integrated teaching: activate prior knowledge, scaffold the language they need to handle the content, then climb from lower- to higher-order thinking within the same lesson.

Why each accessibility item matters

Alt text
Screen readers announce it in place of the image. Describe the information, not the picture — and mark purely decorative images as decorative so they're skipped.
Colour contrast
Aim for a clear difference between text and background — roughly 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text. It matters for low vision, for older screens, and for anyone reading on a phone in sunlight.
Captions and transcripts
Captions serve deaf and hard-of-hearing learners; transcripts also help anyone in a noisy room, anyone who reads faster than you speak, and anyone searching for a phrase later.
Keyboard navigable
Every interaction should work without a mouse, with a visible focus outline. If a drag-and-drop has no keyboard route, it's a locked door.
Plain language
Short sentences, defined jargon, expanded acronyms on first use. This helps second-language learners and, honestly, everyone at 4pm.
Not colour alone
"Click the green button" fails for colour-blind learners. Add a label, an icon, or a position.
Headings and links
Real heading levels let screen-reader users skim. "Read the safety policy" beats "click here" for everyone.
Printable version
Some learners want it on paper, offline, or in front of them while they practise on the real system.
